## 🔬 What's this project actually about?

In one sentence: **we're trying to understand how cyanobacteria affect the connection between aquatic and terrestrial ecosystems.**

Here's the longer, friendlier version:

Lakes and ponds aren't isolated from the land around them — they're constantly exchanging stuff with it. One of the coolest (and most underappreciated) links is **emerging aquatic insects**: insects that spend their larval life underwater and then emerge as adults, flying out of the water and into the terrestrial world. When they emerge, they become food for spiders, birds, bats, and all sorts of land-based predators. In many ecosystems, this pulse of insects is a huge — and often irreplaceable — source of energy and nutrients for terrestrial food webs. Cut off that pulse, or make it low quality, and you can ripple effects all the way up terrestrial food chains, even if nothing "bad" seems to be happening in the lake itself.

Now bring in **cyanobacteria** (aka "blue-green algae"). Cyanobacterial blooms are becoming more common worldwide due to warming and nutrient pollution, and they can mess with aquatic food webs in (at least) two very different ways:

1. **Toxins** — many cyanobacteria produce toxins (like microcystins) that can directly harm the animals that eat them.
2. **Poor food quality** — even when they're not toxic, cyanobacteria tend to be nutritionally poor food (they lack certain essential fatty acids and other nutrients that animals need to grow and reproduce).

The tricky part: these two effects usually happen *at the same time* in nature, so it's really hard to know which one is actually driving the damage. Is it the poison, or is it the "junk food" effect (or both)? **Teasing these two mechanisms apart is a central goal of this project.**

## 🧪 Why Chaoborus (the phantom midge)?

To study this, we use *Chaoborus* — commonly known as the **phantom midge** — as our model organism. A few reasons it's a great fit:

- It's a genus that's **widespread around the world**, found in lakes and ponds across many continents, so what we learn has broad relevance.
- Its larvae live in the water and its adults emerge into the air, making it a perfect messenger for studying the aquatic–terrestrial link described above.
- It's a well-established model in freshwater ecology, so there's a solid foundation of methods and knowledge to build on.

By feeding *Chaoborus* different cyanobacterial diets (toxic vs. non-toxic, nutritionally varied, etc.) and tracking how they grow, survive, and eventually emerge, we can start to disentangle *toxicity* from *food quality* — and understand what that means for the animals waiting on land for their next meal to fly out of the water.
